What Does the Autonomic Nervous System Control?

Abstract

The autonomic nervous system helps regulate heart rate, blood pressure, digestion, body temperature and sweating, pupil responses, and bladder function—usually without any conscious effort on your part.

Your heart doesn't need a fresh command to keep beating.

Eat a meal, and your stomach and intestines start moving on their own. Step into the heat, and your body begins to sweat before you've decided to do anything about it. Step into bright light, and your pupils shrink without any conscious effort. Your bladder quietly stores urine for hours, then lets it go at a convenient moment.

All of this happens because of the autonomic nervous system — the part of the nervous system that helps regulate many bodily functions you don't consciously direct.

It contributes to the control of:

  • Heart rate and blood pressure
  • Digestion and intestinal movement
  • Body-temperature regulation and sweating
  • Pupil size and how the eyes respond to light
  • Storing and releasing urine

But the autonomic nervous system doesn't run any of these functions by itself.

The brain and spinal cord, hormones, sensory and motor nerves, the digestive tract's own nervous system, and control mechanisms within individual organs all work together with it.

Heart Rate and Blood Pressure

If your heart beat at exactly the same rate whether you were resting or climbing a flight of stairs, your body couldn't keep up with its own demands.

The autonomic nervous system helps adjust:

  • How fast the heart beats
  • Some aspects of how forcefully it contracts
  • The tone of your blood vessels
  • Heart-rate and blood-pressure responses to changes in posture

Stand up quickly, for instance, and gravity pulls blood toward your legs. In response, the body adjusts heart rate and vessel tone to help keep blood pressure — and blood flow to the brain — steady.

This response depends on more than the autonomic nervous system alone. Blood volume, heart function, hormones, kidney function, and medications all play a part too.

That means palpitations, dizziness, fainting, or swings in blood pressure don't automatically point to an autonomic problem.

Similar symptoms can come from dehydration, anemia, an irregular heart rhythm, thyroid disease, anxiety, or medication side effects.

Digestion and Intestinal Movement

Moving food through the stomach and intestines takes carefully coordinated muscle contractions and secretions.

The autonomic nervous system contributes to:

  • Movement of the stomach and intestines
  • Release of digestive secretions
  • How quickly food moves through the digestive tract
  • Some of the reflexes involved in bowel movements

The digestive tract also has its own nervous system, called the enteric nervous system.

It regulates much of the movement and secretion within the gastrointestinal tract while staying in constant communication with the sympathetic, parasympathetic, and central nervous systems.

So it isn't accurate to say that digestion simply switches on when the parasympathetic system is active and shuts off when the sympathetic system takes over.

Food, digestive hormones, stress, sleep, medications, and gastrointestinal conditions all shape how well digestion works.

Indigestion, constipation, or diarrhea, on their own, don't confirm that something is wrong with your autonomic nervous system.

Body Temperature and Sweating

Temperature regulation is coordinated largely by the hypothalamus, a small region deep in the brain, working together with several body systems.

When your body heats up, the autonomic nervous system helps carry out the response — mainly through your sweat glands and the blood vessels in your skin — to help release heat.

In a cold environment, the body works to reduce the heat it loses through the skin instead.

Sweating isn't determined only by exercise or effort. It's shaped by:

  • Ambient temperature and humidity
  • Physical activity
  • Stress and emotion
  • Fever and hormonal changes
  • Medications
  • Neurological or systemic conditions

Sweating too much or too little can occur with autonomic dysfunction.

But a change in sweating by itself isn't enough to diagnose an autonomic disorder or any specific condition.

Pupil Responses

Your pupils widen and narrow depending on how much light is hitting your eyes.

  • In bright light, they typically get smaller.
  • In dim light, they typically get larger.

Sympathetic pathways mainly drive pupil dilation; parasympathetic pathways mainly drive pupil constriction.

Pupil size and responses can also be affected by medications, eye drops, and the condition of the eyes and nervous system.

Some people naturally have a slight, lifelong difference between their two pupils.

That said, seek prompt medical evaluation if a new difference in pupil size appears suddenly, or if one pupil becomes abnormally larger or smaller along with:

  • Severe headache or eye pain
  • Double vision
  • A drooping eyelid
  • A sudden change in vision
  • Altered consciousness
  • Sudden difficulty speaking
  • Weakness or numbness in the face, arm, or leg on one side
  • A recent head injury

Don't wait these symptoms out and assume they're just a simple autonomic imbalance.

Bladder Function

The bladder has to store urine for a period of time, then release it under the right circumstances.

That requires coordination among:

  • The bladder muscle relaxing to allow filling
  • The outlet staying closed to prevent leaks
  • The bladder contracting during urination
  • The urethra and pelvic-floor muscles relaxing appropriately
  • Sensory signals telling the brain that the bladder is filling

The autonomic nervous system contributes to this process, but it doesn't control urination on its own.

The brain, spinal cord, sensory nerves, motor nerves, the urinary sphincter, and pelvic-floor muscles are all involved as well.

Symptoms such as the following can have many possible causes:

  • Frequent urination
  • A sudden, strong urge to urinate
  • Urinary leakage
  • Difficulty urinating
  • A sense of incomplete emptying

Urinary-tract infection, prostate conditions, pelvic-floor dysfunction, diabetes, medications, and other urological or neurological conditions all need to be considered.

Urinary symptoms alone cannot diagnose an autonomic disorder.

Is the Sympathetic Nervous System Bad and the Parasympathetic Nervous System Good?

No.

The sympathetic nervous system is a normal, necessary system for responding to exercise, changes in posture, and stressful or demanding situations.

Depending on the organ and circumstances, it may contribute to heart rate, blood-vessel tone, pupil dilation, sweating, and urine storage.

The parasympathetic nervous system plays an important role in resting heart-rate regulation, digestive movement and secretion, and bladder emptying.

So statements like these are oversimplified:

  • The sympathetic nervous system is bad.
  • The parasympathetic nervous system is good.
  • Lowering sympathetic activity improves every symptom.
  • Raising parasympathetic activity restores the autonomic nervous system.

The sympathetic and parasympathetic systems don't function like a single on/off switch.

Both are necessary for normal physiology, and they work differently depending on the organ and the situation.

Healthy autonomic function is less about permanently favoring one branch and more about the ability to produce an appropriate response for what the body needs at the time.

Do These Symptoms Mean You Have Autonomic Dysfunction?

Symptoms that can occur with autonomic dysfunction include:

  • Severe dizziness when standing
  • Fainting
  • Abnormal heart-rate or blood-pressure responses
  • Changes in sweating
  • Digestive-motility problems
  • Changes in bladder function
  • Abnormal pupil responses

But none of these symptoms are specific to autonomic nervous system disorders.

Similar symptoms can show up with:

  • Anemia and dehydration
  • Abnormal heart rhythms and other cardiovascular conditions
  • Thyroid disease
  • Infection
  • Medications
  • Anxiety
  • Insufficient sleep
  • Gastrointestinal disease
  • Urological conditions
  • Hormonal changes
  • Other neurological or systemic conditions

So symptoms alone are not enough to conclude that:

  • Your sympathetic nervous system is overactive.
  • Your parasympathetic nervous system is weak.
  • Your autonomic balance has broken down.
  • Your autonomic nervous system is damaged.

What matters is when the symptoms began, how they've progressed, whether they relate to posture, exercise, or meals, what medications you take, other symptoms you're experiencing, and what other explanations are possible.

Can Autonomic Testing Identify the Cause?

Autonomic function tests can help assess specific physiological responses, such as heart-rate and blood-pressure changes with posture, cardiovascular responses to controlled breathing, and sweating responses.

But no single test can determine:

  • The cause of every symptom
  • Your overall sympathetic–parasympathetic "balance"
  • One specific diagnosis
  • Whether heart, endocrine, gastrointestinal, or urinary disease has been ruled out
  • What treatment you need

Whether testing is needed depends on your symptoms and a clinician's judgment.

Depending on your history and examination, a clinician may selectively consider:

  • Heart rate and blood pressure measured lying down and standing up
  • An electrocardiogram
  • History-guided blood testing
  • Evaluation of a related organ system
  • Select autonomic function tests

An abnormal result doesn't automatically confirm one cause.

A normal result also doesn't mean a patient's symptoms aren't real.

Results need to be interpreted together with the medical history, physical examination, and any other appropriate testing.

When to See a Doctor

Consider a medical evaluation if the following symptoms recur or interfere with everyday activities:

  • Repeated severe dizziness when standing
  • An episode of fainting
  • Recurring, unexplained palpitations
  • A sweating pattern that has changed significantly
  • Persistent, severe digestive or bladder problems
  • Symptoms that began after starting a new medication or being diagnosed with a condition
  • New or progressive numbness, weakness, or changes in how you walk
  • A change in pupil response or vision
  • Symptoms that create safety concerns during driving, exercise, or daily activities

Fainting deserves a workup in particular, since it can result from heart conditions and other medical causes, not only autonomic ones.

When to Seek Emergency Care

Don't wait and assume the following symptoms are caused by an autonomic problem. Call 911 in the United States—or your local emergency number—right away if you experience:

  • Chest pain
  • Severe shortness of breath
  • Fainting during exercise
  • Loss of consciousness that doesn't resolve quickly, or a slow recovery
  • Fainting along with ongoing, severe palpitations
  • Sudden difficulty speaking or understanding speech
  • Sudden weakness or numbness in the face, arm, or leg, especially on one side
  • Sudden vision changes
  • Sudden difficulty walking or loss of balance
  • A seizure
  • A sudden, severe headache with no clear cause
  • A sudden difference in pupil size along with headache, double vision, a drooping eyelid, or altered consciousness

Even if a sudden neurological symptom improves on its own, it still needs immediate evaluation.

Key Takeaway

The autonomic nervous system helps regulate the following functions so they can respond to changing conditions without requiring your constant attention:

  • Heart rate and blood pressure
  • Digestion and intestinal movement
  • Body-temperature regulation and sweating
  • Pupil responses
  • Urine storage and bladder emptying

It doesn't do this alone.

The brain, spinal cord, the digestive tract's own nervous system, hormones, sensory and motor nerves, and each organ's local regulation all work together with it.

Both the sympathetic and parasympathetic systems are necessary for normal physiology.

Palpitations, dizziness, digestive symptoms, sweating changes, or urinary symptoms cannot diagnose autonomic dysfunction on their own.

If symptoms persist or get worse, a medical evaluation that considers both autonomic and non-autonomic causes may be needed.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is high sympathetic activity always a bad thing?

No.

Sympathetic activity is a normal, necessary response to exercise, standing up, and demanding situations.

A symptom, or even a single test, isn't enough to conclude that your sympathetic nervous system is generally "too high."

Will boosting parasympathetic activity fix every symptom?

Not necessarily.

The parasympathetic system matters for resting heart rate, digestion, and bladder function.

But it isn't accurate to blame every symptom on low parasympathetic activity, or to claim that a particular breathing technique, device, food, or supplement will treat symptoms or restore the autonomic nervous system.

Are palpitations or indigestion always an autonomic problem?

Autonomic changes can be one possible explanation.

But palpitations can also stem from arrhythmias, anemia, dehydration, thyroid disease, medications, or anxiety.

Indigestion can be driven by food, medications, stress, or a range of gastrointestinal conditions.

Symptoms alone can't tell you which explanation applies.

Can a single autonomic test provide a diagnosis?

No single test can confirm the cause of every symptom or identify one specific condition.

Autonomic function tests are a supporting tool—their usefulness, and what the results mean, depend on the medical history, physical examination, and any other testing involved.
